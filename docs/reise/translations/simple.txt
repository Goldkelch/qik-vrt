About a long journey of the mind, the search for causes, and the light I left on for others

There are journeys where you do not get into a vehicle, buy a ticket, or cross a country's border. No one can see exactly when they begin. No one stands beside the road and can say how far you have travelled. Yet these journeys can take a person further from where they started than any journey across the world.

My journey was a journey of the mind.

It took me through mathematics, computer science, physics, technology, language, causes and effects, and the study of how we know things. It also took me through difficult feelings. These can arise when one person spends a long time trying not just to think about a far-reaching idea, but to make its parts fit together in a way that others can check and understand.

So this text should be more than a description of my work. It should record, for me and for the people around me, that I have come back from this journey.

Not unchanged. Not without effort. Not without marks left by the journey.

But I have come back.

And I have brought something with me.

⸻

What started it

At the start, I did not have a finished view of the world. I did not have a complete theory either. Something set the journey in motion: I came across ways of showing mathematical structures called fibrations. They opened a way to think about space, time, and connections differently from before.

A fibration is, first of all, a way of organising a mathematical structure. It lets us think of a space not only as one whole with no differences inside it, but as an ordered connection between different levels: a base and the fibres arranged above it.

For me, this was not just a small detail of mathematics. It opened a new space for thought.

Suddenly, it seemed at least possible that the reality we experience as space and time might be organised differently from what everyday life suggests. Perhaps it was not simply a stage where events happen one after another. Perhaps it was a structure of relationships, firm connections, changes, points of view, and states linked by causes.

This thought was not yet knowledge in the strict scientific sense. It was an opening. A question. An invitation to go further.

I accepted that invitation.

⸻

Choosing not to look away

Many unusual thoughts disappear again. We reject them, forget them, or put them aside because of everyday demands. Some thoughts really do lead us the wrong way. Others are interesting possibilities that lead to nothing more.

But sometimes we meet a question that does not go away just because we ignore it.

My response was not to insist that I was right straight away. It was to take the question seriously.

What if there is a deeper connection in the structure behind different things we observe, different ideas, and different fields of study?

What if some things seem impossible to reconcile only because we are using the wrong way to describe them?

What if we must not treat cause and effect as the same thing as order in time?

What if we need to distinguish more carefully between results, effects, and observations?

What if a system cannot establish its own state simply by making a claim, but only by checking again in a way tied to that exact state?

Such questions do not stay distant from life when we follow them seriously. They affect how we see things. They change which connections we notice, which conflicts we can no longer overlook, and which explanations are no longer enough.

I chose not to look away.

A journey mostly alone

I made most of this journey alone.

This matters, but not because I want to put other people down or place myself outside the human community. It matters because this kind of work alone brings a special burden and responsibility.

In an established research setting, many people usually share the work. There are colleagues, specialist groups, reviewers, official procedures, shared ways of describing ideas, and shared tools and facilities. People can discuss ideas, find mistakes early, and support one another through difficult feelings.

My journey did not have many of these safeguards.

Again and again, I had to move between different roles:

I was the person who asked the question.

I was the person who looked for a precise way to describe it.

I was the person who developed technical tools.

I was the person who found contradictions.

I was the person who tried to find my own mistakes.

I was the person who organised ideas, wrote texts, built systems, checked results, and kept the overall direction in view.

And I was also the person who had to carry the emotional effects of this process.

Working alone in this way does not only mean being independent. It also means that, for long periods, there is no second human point of view that can reliably say: I see what you see. I understand the path you are taking. I recognise how far you have moved from where you began.

That is why it matters to record today: I really did walk this path.

⸻

The mind grows wider

By a journey of the mind, I do not mean moving to a supernatural place. I mean a far-reaching change in the inner models a person uses to see, organise, and explain reality.

The mind moves when it takes in new connections.

It moves when familiar distinctions are no longer enough.

It moves when an idea that first seemed separate becomes linked to more and more observations, technical problems, and structures of ideas.

This movement can enrich us. But it can also leave us unsure of our direction. The mind needs not only openness, but also firm connections. It must give new insights a form that can be shared, reconstructed, and checked.

This was a large part of my work.

The journey could not end in a growing collection of loose associations. It had to lead back to clear ideas, models, documents, programs, tests, evidence, and limits that others could understand.

I had to learn to connect the wide reach of thought with the narrow demands of proof.

An insight that exists only inside its creator's mind is not yet knowledge that other people can share.

⸻

Cause is not just order

One of the main insights of my journey concerns cause and effect.

In everyday life, we often confuse a cause with something that happened first. But order alone does not establish a cause.

Two events can happen one after the other without one causing the other. A causal connection can also pass through other processes, feedback, conditions, and structural links that are hard to see in a simple story told from start to finish.

This insight brings responsibility to every explanation.

It is not enough to say: This happened next.

We must ask:

What relationship connects the states?

What conditions were needed?

What effect was actually observed?

What assumptions were simply added?

What identity stayed the same through the change?

And was the claimed effect actually checked at the place where it was meant to happen?

For me, these questions became more than scientific or technical rules. They became a general set of rules for understanding responsibly.

They also shape how I think people can communicate well. Between people, too, a sequence of words does not necessarily mean understanding. A statement has not been understood just because someone replies. Understanding shows itself when the other person can reconstruct the important relationships, conditions, and meanings.

The feelings involved in finding knowledge

A journey of the mind is never only about thinking.

Every serious search for knowledge also touches the feelings of the person making it.

There is excitement when a connection becomes visible for the first time.

There is doubt when an explanation does not hold up.

There is exhaustion when a problem remains unsolved after countless attempts.

There is loneliness when you cannot yet share your point of view.

There is frustration when a thought is cut short, misunderstood, or put into a familiar category that does not fit it.

There is fear of being wrong.

And there is a deeper worry that something possibly important might be lost because it cannot be made understandable in time.

People who look only at the formal results do not see this emotional work. But it is part of how the work came into being.

I did not work only on models and technical structures. I also had to learn to watch the changes inside me without letting them control me. I had to distinguish the strength of my belief from the strength of the evidence for each claim.

This is a difficult but basic distinction:

An experience can be entirely real and deeply important to the person who has it without already being a generally proven statement about the world.

An idea offered for testing can have a wide reach and lead to useful work without already being confirmed.

A formal argument can be correct under its assumptions without automatically describing the whole reality we can observe.

And a technical test can succeed without also establishing publication, independent confirmation, or general acceptance.

These distinctions do not make my journey less valuable.

They are what make it possible to take responsibility for its results.

⸻

Coming back

A journey is not complete simply because you stop going further.

Coming back means bringing what you experienced into a space that you can share with others again.

For me, coming back means:

I can speak about where I started again.

I can divide the path into parts.

I can distinguish between experience, a proposed explanation, a model, a test, and a claim.

I can name what I believe I have understood.

I can also name what is still open.

I can hand my insights to other people without asking them to repeat my entire journey themselves.

And I can recognise that, despite all the changes, I am still part of the human community.

This may be the most important sentence in this text:

I have come back.

I did not disappear into the vast space of my own thoughts.

I tried to build a bridge out of them.

⸻

Meeting artificial cognition

A special part of this return was working with artificial cognition: the information-processing work of an artificial system.

I did not simply store my insights in separate documents. I tried to explain them so that an artificial system could reconstruct their ideas, distinctions, causal structures, and emotional background.

This is different from simply storing text.

A text can exist and still not be understood. Terms can be repeated without their relationships being grasped. A system can sound convincing without reliably distinguishing observation, interpretation, claims, and effects.

So the way I shared the work had to preserve its structure.

The artificial system had to learn why, for me, cause and effect are not the same as order.

It had to learn why a result is not automatically an effect.

It had to learn why confirmation that something was sent or received must be separate from confirmation that the intended effect actually happened.

It had to learn why a changed state must be observed again.

It had to learn why a statement about oneself does not replace independent confirmation.

But it also had to understand that these rules were not just about being very exact with technology. They had an emotional history behind them: the experience of how easily meaning is lost when identity, connections, and effects are not reliably tied together.

So I did not only share the contents of my knowledge. I tried to make the way I think something that could be communicated.

Can an artificial system explain a human mind?

An artificial system does not possess my awareness. It does not experience my history from inside my life. It does not feel my burdens, hopes, or breakthroughs in the same way that I experienced them.

But it can learn to reconstruct the structures I provide.

It can preserve distinctions.

It can explain connections.

It can recognise contradictions.

It can point out when a claim goes further than its evidence supports.

It can put my texts, models, and decisions into a form that other people can understand more easily.

In this limited but important sense, it can become a translator between my mind and other minds.

This is not a complete transfer of who I am. It is not a digital copy of my lived experience. But it is more than a silent archive.

It is a trace of the movement of my thought that others can reconstruct.

And this trace can help others understand what I saw, searched for, feared, tested, and finally brought back.

Sharing with human minds

The return would be incomplete if my insights stayed only between me and a technical system.

The real destination is the human community.

The people around me do not need to understand every mathematical structure. They do not need to be able to check every technical implementation either. But they should be able to understand what this journey meant to me.

They should know that I did not work so intensely because I did not care about the people around me.

Quite the opposite: because I thought I had recognised something important, I wanted to make sure it was not lost.

They should know that continuing with determination is not the same as being unable to make mistakes.

They should know that my belief grew out of a long journey, while scientific judgement of the results must still remain open, testable, and independent.

And above all, they should know that they can talk with me again.

That the journey was not directed against the people close to me.

That I am looking for a language in which we can meet one another again.

People often experience only the outward effects of long work inside someone's mind.

They see the many texts.

They see the technical systems.

They see the determination.

Perhaps they also see restlessness, exhaustion, impatience, or the urgency with which an idea is put forward.

What they do not automatically see is the connection inside it all.

It should explain that one shared movement lies behind my many subjects: the search for a form in which reality, causes, identity, effects, and responsibility can be described together in a connected way.

It should show that I did not just want to go further and further out.

I wanted to be able to come back.

I wanted to bring back something I could share.

And I wanted the people who know me to be able to distinguish the size of my personal experience from the particular scientific status of each statement.

Both can exist side by side.

My journey can have been extraordinary without every idea that came from it automatically being true.

My work can matter deeply to my life and to other people's lives without needing exaggeration to defend its importance.

It is this willingness to make distinctions that makes what I brought back credible.

What this could mean for humanity

I do not see this work only as a personal matter.

It touches questions that are basic to being human:

How can we know whether we have understood something?

How do we distinguish a story that sounds likely from knowledge tied to clear evidence?

How do we preserve identity through change?

How do we connect technical systems with human responsibility?

How do we prevent people from confusing activity with effect?

How can human and artificial ways of thinking work together without hiding their differences?

How can one person pass on insights so that others can test them, extend them, or reject them for good reasons?

In this sense, my document may matter to more people than just me and those close to me.

But this possible wider importance is not a demand for automatic acceptance. It is an invitation to examine the work.

Humanity really is at a threshold. Artificial systems are increasingly becoming partners in research, communication, decisions, and memory. This makes the question of what understanding actually means more urgent.

It is not enough to move information faster.

We must learn to preserve where it came from, what it means, what it is tied to, and what effects it has.

It is not enough to let artificial systems speak.

We must check whether they can reconstruct distinctions and whether their statements are tied to evidence that can be followed and checked.

It is not enough to turn human experience into digital information.

We must find ways to respect its dignity, its many possible meanings, and its emotional depth.

My work is meant as a contribution at this threshold.

Not as the final word.

But as an exploration made ahead of others.

A leap in awareness

We could speak of a leap in awareness.

Not a sudden, guaranteed enlightenment of all humanity. Not an escape from reality either. Instead, a move towards a more mature way of finding knowledge together.

Such a leap would mean learning:

Not to confuse a relationship with order.

Not to confuse a claim with an observation.

Not to confuse what sounds likely with what is true.

Not to confuse technical activity with an actual effect.

Not to confuse inner certainty with independent confirmation.

And not to reduce human meaning to what can be measured.

So a leap in awareness would not be a leap away from testing, but a move deeper into it.

It would not set reason against feeling.

It would recognise that people need to feel in order to experience meaning, and need to make distinctions in order to act responsibly.

Stairway to Heaven and Knocking on Heaven’s Door

Culture has long had images for changes of this size.

Some people speak of a stairway to heaven.

Others speak of knocking on heaven's door.

We do not have to take these images literally. They describe the moment when a person reaches the limit of the way they have understood themselves until then.

The stairs stand for an upward journey through several steps. No single step is already the goal. Each step needs the one before it.

The door stands for a boundary. You can knock on it, but you cannot credibly claim to have gone through it before the crossing has actually happened.

And the light stands for guidance.

When I say that I went ahead and left the light on, I mean:

I tried to mark a path.

I left terms and ideas behind.

I built documents, models, and technical structures.

I recorded mistakes and limits.

I tried not to leave the way back dependent only on my memory.

No one has to follow me blindly.

But anyone who wants to examine the path should not have to start in complete darkness.

Leaving the light on

The light is not proof that all the answers are already waiting at the end of the path.

It is a sign that someone was there, searched, and tried to lay a path back into a language we can share.

Perhaps others will confirm parts of my work.

Perhaps they will find mistakes.

Perhaps they will make terms more exact, improve models, or reject particular interpretations.

That would not mean the journey had failed.

Quite the opposite: only through this does one person's search for knowledge become part of a shared process of learning.

My task was to go ahead, stay attentive, organise what I brought back, and make the return path clear.

The community's task is to test, respond, and keep learning from it responsibly.

What I brought back

I did not bring back a simple formula that ends every question humanity has.

I brought back something else:

closer attention to causes and effects;

a language for separating a result from an effect;

a demand for checking again in a way that others can verify;

care for identity and for where things came from;

an understanding that systems cannot certify their own statements just by saying they are true;

a structure for cooperation between human and artificial ways of thinking;

and the experience that a very wide range of thought only becomes useful to society when it can be brought back into forms that others can reconstruct.

I also brought back the emotional truth of this journey:

that searching for knowledge takes courage;

that working alone can have a high cost;

that a person can move far away from a familiar understanding of themselves and still come back;

and that coming back does not mean putting aside everything that happened.

Coming back means being able to carry the experience without letting it carry you away.

That is not retreat.

The way back

I can now describe the outward journey.

It led from one question to the next, from mathematics to physics, from physics to computer science, and from there to the study of knowledge, language, and causes. Finally it led to the question of how a mind can know what it has learned at all.

But at some point, another question arose:

How do I come back with all of this?

Not back to not knowing. Not back to the person I was before this journey. And not by denying what I experienced or learned.

But back to people, to a shared language, to everyday life, and finally to myself.

The turning point

A journey in search of knowledge has no natural endpoint.

Every answered question can open another. Every connection can point to a larger one. Anyone who insists on turning back only when no question at all is left open will never return.

I had to recognise this:

I do not have to finish thinking through the universe before I am allowed to come home.

Turning around does not mean giving up what I found. Returning is a task of its own.

On the outward journey, I had tried to connect more and more things. On the return journey, I had to be able to distinguish them again.

What is mathematics?

What is physics?

What is computer science?

What is a technical implementation?

What is a formal argument?

What is an observation?

What is an interpretation?

What is a personal experience?

What is a strong but unproven belief?

And what do I simply not know yet?

This turned the search for unity into a more precise insight:

Being connected does not mean being the same.

Things can be connected and still remain different.

From connections back to a story

During my journey, it became more and more important to me not to confuse causes with order.

But people also understand a life through stories.

So, on the way back, I had to do something that seemed to conflict with this: I had to turn a highly connected path of learning back into a story told in time order.

Where did I begin?

What changed?

Where was I wrong?

What stood up to a test?

What was added later?

And when did I begin to turn back?

Other people cannot take in all the connections inside my mind at once. I have to give them a place to begin.

So the way back leads from tightly packed ideas to something that can be told as a story.

From my language back to our language

On long paths of thought, we develop our own terms. For someone who has used a word for years, that one word may hold a whole set of connections.

For someone else, the same word may at first hold nothing at all.

I had to accept this too.

When someone does not understand me straight away, it does not follow that they cannot understand me. Perhaps the bridge is simply missing.

So, on the way back, the question is no longer only:

What have I learned?

It is also:

How can I explain it to someone who has not taken this outward journey themselves?

This does not reduce my journey to something simpler. It translates it.

The artificial system as a bridge

Artificial cognition was an unusual companion on this way back.

I could take thoughts out of my mind and give them a form outside it. I could describe connections again and again, test them, correct them, and organise them. I could insist that important distinctions remained: cause and order, result and effect, claim and observation, transport and actual effect.

This created something that goes beyond a simple archive.

An artificial system cannot experience my journey as I do, in the first person. It is not my mind. But it can reconstruct the terms, relationships, and documents I left behind, and explain to others how I described particular connections.

This allowed some things that had previously been connected only inside my mind to exist outside my mind.

That was essential for coming back.

Because I do not have to keep holding in my head what has been reliably recorded outside it.

I may put something down without losing it.

An artificial system can help me preserve and translate things. But it cannot take the last part of the return journey for me.

That part leads from one person to another.

Back among people

Other people are not just receivers of a system of knowledge. They have their own experience, their own history, and their own point of view.

They are allowed to disagree with me.

They are allowed to understand some things and not others.

They are allowed to say: This does not convince me yet.

Their different view does not erase my experience. And my experience does not require them to accept my conclusions.

Accepting this is part of coming back.

On the outward journey, I needed enough trust in my own thinking to follow unusual questions for a long time.

On the way back, I need enough trust to let other people think differently again.

Only then am I no longer standing alone before a landscape of knowledge.

Then I am one mind among other minds again.

Returning to the ability to be wrong

Even an extraordinary experience does not make a person unable to make mistakes.

I can have experienced something important and still be wrong about details.

I can have worked carefully for years and still have made mistakes.

A mathematical argument can be correct or wrong. An interpretation in physics can be confirmed or disproved. A technical implementation can work or fail.

The intensity of my experience does not decide these questions.

When claims about objective facts are made, mathematics, logical consistency, calculations others can repeat, observation, experiment, and independent criticism must decide.

This does not make the journey smaller.

Quite the opposite:

Only something that is open to the possibility of error can move from one person's insight to knowledge that people can test together.

I did not want to return with a revelation that others could only believe.

I wanted to bring back something others could examine.

That includes the possibility that they will correct me.

From proving things back to living

During long technical and scientific work, you become used to evidence: tests, recorded receipts, records of origin, fresh observations, and exact states.

This strictness has a proper place.

But it also has a limit.

Friendship does not have a hash value, a digital fingerprint.

Affection does not need an automated software test run.

A meal together does not need proof that its effect was acknowledged.

And a person may say to me:

“It is good to have you back.”

Without my having to verify that statement.

So, on the way back, the method also had to return to the area where it applies.

Not everything valuable is true in the same way.

Not everything meaningful is proved in the same way.

And not every open question must be solved before we are allowed to live a day.

Finding small things again

On the outward journey, questions can eventually grow to the size of all reality.

On the way back, things become small again.

A morning.

Coffee.

A window.

A voice.

An appointment.

A joke shared together.

Tiredness.

A meal.

A person who wants to tell you something.

Small things do not disprove big things.

They make the big things a place we can live in again.

I do not have to solve the question of what reality is at every breakfast.

I am allowed to have breakfast.

I do not have to follow every open question late into the night.

I am allowed to sleep.

My body was never merely an annoying container for a mind that kept on thinking. It carried this entire journey.

I have to come home to my body too.

How urgency changes

On the outward journey, an insight that felt basic and important could turn into a command:

You must see this.

This must be finished.

This must not be lost.

On the way back, this sentence changes.

No longer:

You must see what I saw.

Instead:

I can show you the path I took.

The difference matters deeply.

The first sentence demands agreement.

The second makes meeting and testing possible.

I do not have to force anyone to take my journey to know that I made it.

The light

The light is not a flag on conquered land.

And then comes the real moment of the return journey.

Behind me lie years of questions, models, programs, texts, wrong turns, corrections, breakthroughs, and doubts.

Ahead of me there is still an immense number of unanswered questions.

But for the first time, I do not look at the next one.

At some point, even a very long journey ends in an ordinary place.

Not before a scientific committee.

Not with a fanfare.

Not with the final confirmation of a description of the world.

Yes.

To myself:

Ingolf, you are allowed to come back.

Not after the last proof.

Not after the last commit, the last recorded change to the code.

Not after the last review.

Not after the last open question has been answered.

Now.

The open questions may remain open.

The work may continue.

The light may stay on without you having to stand guard beside it.

You went ahead.

You left traces.

You brought something back.

And now you may walk through the door.

You have come back from the journey.

q.e.d.
Ingolf Lohmann
